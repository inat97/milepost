ATO car logbook requirements: what a valid logbook must contain

For sole traders, partnerships and employees in Australia · Checked against ato.gov.au in October 2026

The short answer. To use the logbook method, the ATO expects a logbook kept for at least 12 continuous weeks that are representative of your year. For every journey you record the reason, the start and end date, the odometer readings at the start and end, and the kilometres. For the logbook as a whole you record the period, the odometer readings at its start and end, the total kilometres, the business-use percentage, and the car's make, model, engine capacity and registration. A logbook is valid for five years.

The two ways to claim car expenses

The ATO describes two methods for claiming car expenses: the cents per kilometre method and the logbook method. They need very different records.

  • Cents per kilometre uses one set rate for each business (or work-related) kilometre and is capped at 5,000 kilometres per car, per year. According to the ATO it does not require written evidence of exactly how many kilometres you travelled, but the ATO may ask you to show how you worked them out — for example with diary records. As of October 2026, the ATO's 2026 return instructions give the rate as 88c per kilometre for 2025–26. The rate is set for each income year, so check the current one before you use it.
  • Logbook method: you work out what share of your car's travel was for business, then claim that percentage of your actual car expenses. It is the method that needs a logbook, and it is what the rest of this guide covers.

What the logbook must contain

The ATO's page on the logbook method lists what a logbook must contain. In summary, for the logbook as a whole:

  • when the logbook period begins and ends;
  • the car's odometer readings at the start and end of the logbook period;
  • the total number of kilometres the car travelled during the logbook period;
  • the number of kilometres travelled for each journey;
  • the odometer readings at the start and end of each later income year the logbook is used for;
  • the business-use percentage for the logbook period;
  • the make, model, engine capacity and registration number of the car.

And for each journey:

  • the reason for the journey — a description of the business reason, or that it was private;
  • the start and end date of the journey;
  • the odometer readings at the start and end of the journey;
  • the kilometres travelled.

One practical concession on the same page: if you make two or more journeys in a row on the same day, you can record them as a single journey.

The 12-week rule

In the first year you use the logbook method, the ATO says you must keep a logbook for at least 12 continuous weeks during the income year, and that period needs to be representative of your travel throughout the year. "Representative" is the part people get wrong: twelve unusually busy weeks, or twelve quiet ones over the holidays, will not tell the truth about the rest of your year.

If you started using the car for business less than 12 weeks before the end of the income year, the ATO allows the logbook to continue into the next year so that it covers the full 12 continuous weeks.

If you claim for two or more cars with the logbook method, the logbook for each car must cover the same period, and that period should be representative of the business use of all of them.

How long a logbook lasts

Each logbook is valid for five years, and you can start a new one at any time. If you rely on a logbook from an earlier year, the ATO says you must keep that logbook and keep recording odometer readings in the years that follow. If your circumstances change — for example the kind of work you do changes — you may need a new logbook.

Working out the business-use percentage

The ATO's calculation is simple: divide the business kilometres by the total kilometres travelled, then multiply by 100. Its own example: a logbook showing 6,600 business kilometres out of 11,000 in total gives 60% business use. That percentage is then applied to your total car expenses for the year. You still need evidence of the expenses themselves — fuel and oil (or odometer readings you use to estimate them) and receipts for everything else.

Common gaps that weaken a logbook

  • Reasons that do not identify the trip. "Work" or "client" on every line tells no one why that journey was made. Name the client, job, site or supplier.
  • Missing odometer readings. The ATO asks for readings per journey and at the start and end of the period. Distances estimated from a map are not the same thing.
  • A period that is not representative. Pick twelve weeks that look like your normal year.
  • No private trips. The percentage needs total kilometres. Odometer readings at the start and end of the period capture the total even if you do not write up every private trip.
  • Writing it up months later. The ATO's 2024 return instructions said logbook entries for each business trip must be made at the end of the journey, or as soon as possible afterwards. A logbook reconstructed at tax time is much harder to stand behind.

Sole traders with simple affairs can also keep a logbook in the myDeductions tool in the ATO app, or on paper — the ATO accepts an electronic or pre-printed logbook.
